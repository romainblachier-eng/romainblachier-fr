I wrote an op-ed for **[CommonWealth Magazine](https://english.cw.com.tw/article/article.action?id=4772)** (天下雜誌) — in its *Insight / Op-eds* section — on a variable too often overlooked in Taiwan's energy debate: not nuclear power or renewables, but **the price of electricity itself**.

## What is CommonWealth Magazine?

**CommonWealth Magazine** (天下雜誌, *Tianxia*) is Taiwan's flagship economic publication. Founded in 1981 by journalist **Diane Ying** (殷允芃) in the wake of Washington's de-recognition of Taipei, it became the island's most influential title on macroeconomics, industry, technology, geopolitics and society. In 2007 it became the first financial news outlet in Taiwan to launch an English-language edition (*english.cw.com.tw*), aimed at informing an international readership about Taiwan and the greater Chinese world. To publish there is to speak directly to Taiwan's economic and political decision-makers.

## The argument: a frozen price, an unfinanced freeze

Taiwan has the **third-lowest industrial and fifth-lowest residential electricity rates in the world**. That cheapness is not a market outcome — it is a political choice, made without a financing architecture to match.

The numbers tell the story. Industrial users pay an average of **NT$4.27 per kilowatt-hour**, residential users around **NT$2.77**, while the average generation cost at **Taipower** (台灣電力公司) has stood near **NT$3.8/kWh** since the start of 2025. Every kilowatt-hour sold to a household leaves the utility roughly one Taiwan dollar short. In March 2026, the Electricity Rate Review Committee nonetheless chose to freeze the average rate at **NT$3.78/kWh**, citing Middle East volatility and the need to protect industrial competitiveness.

## A debt, not a subsidy

The cost of that freeze is enormous: **Taipower's accumulated losses reached NT$417.9 billion** (around US$13 billion) by the end of July 2025, despite several rate hikes since 2022 and an NT$72.9 billion post-tax profit in 2024. A NT$100 billion recapitalization package was also blocked in the Legislative Yuan.

That is my central point: the gap between the tariff charged and the true cost **is not a subsidy — it is a debt**. The longer the price-financing gap persists, the more expensive it becomes to close.

## The hidden tax on the transition and AI

Cheap power also weakens the energy transition. Offshore wind, solar and geothermal developers must compete against a regulated grid tariff that does not reflect the true cost of fossil-fuelled marginal generation. Under those conditions, it is hard to negotiate at scale the corporate power purchase agreements (CPPAs) on which the Lai Ching-te administration's 2030 climate targets depend.

And demand pressure will only grow: driven by artificial intelligence, consumption could rise by **12–13% by 2030**, while the grid needs tens of billions of NT dollars in upgrades. A freeze designed to shield industry could, paradoxically, end up undermining the very thing it claims to protect.
