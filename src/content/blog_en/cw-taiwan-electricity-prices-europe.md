I have just published an article on **CommonWealth Magazine** (天下雜誌), in Taipei, on Taiwan's electricity prices and what the European Union's 2024 electricity market reform can bring to the Taiwanese debate.

## CommonWealth Magazine

**CommonWealth Magazine** is one of Taiwan's leading magazines on economic, political and societal issues. Founded in 1981 by Diane Ying, it has become a major venue for serious policy analysis on the island, with an English edition (english.cw.com.tw) that bridges domestic debate and an international readership. Publishing there means speaking directly to the industrial, political and academic circles that shape Taiwan's energy policy.

## The argument: an unfinanced price freeze, and what Europe brings to the table

Taiwan has the **third-lowest industrial and fifth-lowest residential electricity rates in the world**. This is not a market outcome — it is a political choice, made without a financing architecture to match. Taipower's average generation cost stands at NT$3.8 per kWh, while households pay NT$2.77 per kWh. Every kilowatt-hour sold to a household leaves the utility one Taiwan dollar short. The result: **NT$417.9 billion in accumulated losses** by the end of July 2025, despite several rate hikes since 2022.

In the article, I show that this price-cost gap, framed as a tool of industrial competitiveness, **effectively taxes the energy transition**:

- Wind, solar and geothermal developers compete against a regulated tariff that does not reflect the true cost of fossil-fuelled marginal generation.
- The absence of locational signals forces the administration to bluntly cap new data centres above 5 MW north of Taoyuan since August 2023.
- The grid itself is starved of investment, just as AI and semiconductor demand is set to add the equivalent of nearly a Taichung Power Plant by 2030.

This is where the **European toolkit** becomes directly relevant. The EU electricity market reform adopted in May 2024 puts three instruments on the table:

1. **Long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs)** between producers and large consumers — 10 to 15 years — to give renewables the revenue visibility they need to attract investment.
2. **Two-way contracts for difference (CfDs)**, mandatory from 2027 for any direct price support to new investments in wind, solar, geothermal, run-of-river hydro and nuclear — including life-extension of existing reactors. A symmetric mechanism: the producer receives a guaranteed strike price when wholesale prices fall, and returns surplus revenue to consumers when prices rise.
3. **Conditional grid access** for very large consumers, on the Irish model — the 2022–2025 Dublin-region data-centre moratorium, lifted in December 2025 only for operators able to install on-site generation or batteries covering their full demand.

I also recall that a France–Taiwan channel already exists on these issues, anchored in the November 2023 convention between the Bureau Français de Taipei and the BRTF, and most recently expressed by the **2nd Franco-Taiwanese Symposium on Green Energy and Sustainable Industry** at the Université de Lorraine in Nancy on 25–26 November 2025. The channels to translate European tariff and grid experience into Taiwanese policy options are open — and underused.

The window is narrow: AI-driven demand is projected to grow 12–13% by 2030, fuel costs remain exposed to Middle East volatility, and the longer the price–financing gap persists, the more expensive it becomes to close.
