*Lecturer at Sciences Po Paris, EM Lyon, IEP Lyon, ECAM Lyon, ILERI, HEIP · President of the Association France-Formosa*

Taiwan imports 97% of its primary energy. Its strategic reserves of liquefied natural gas cover between 11 and 14 days of consumption. Its last nuclear reactor was shut down in May 2025. And at the same time, TSMC's electricity consumption — the company that manufactures over 90% of the world's most advanced chips — could triple by 2030. The scale of the problem speaks for itself.

## An Electricity Mix on Fossil Life Support

Taiwan's energy profile is one of near-total import dependency. In 2024, liquefied natural gas accounted for 42.7% of electricity production and coal for 33.5%, all of it imported. Renewables had plateaued at 11.6%. The government's so-called "532" target — 50% gas, 30% coal, 20% renewables — has already been pushed back twice and will not be met in 2026.

This dependence on LNG, which enjoys rare cross-party consensus between the DPP and the KMT, is a rational choice in peacetime: gas is cleaner than coal, cheaper to deploy than renewables, and more popular than nuclear. But it is a risky strategic bet once the geopolitical variable is factored in. Supply routes pass through the Strait of Hormuz, the Strait of Malacca, and the South China Sea — all critical chokepoints. The island's three LNG terminals are clustered along the west coast, facing the strait, within direct range of People's Liberation Army missiles.

## Nuclear Power: A Democratic Choice with Strategic Consequences

The shutdown of the Maanshan nuclear plant on May 17, 2025 marked Taiwan's entry into its first nuclear-free summer in four decades. The island's referendum history on this issue deserves closer attention: in 2018, a clear majority voted to keep the plants running, well above the required threshold. The government proceeded with the shutdown regardless. In August 2025, a new referendum on reopening the plants failed to reach the participation threshold, even though an overwhelming majority of those who voted supported reopening.

The result is a paradoxical situation: a public that is largely in favor of nuclear power, but an institutional and political framework that prevents its revival. The DPP, long defined by its anti-nuclear stance as a matter of political identity, has made the exit from nuclear energy an ideological marker. The KMT, which favored keeping the plants open, failed to capitalize on the referendum results. The outcome: a democratic deadlock on a question of national security.

## Renewables: Ambition Constrained by Geography

Taiwan has placed a major bet on offshore wind. The targets are ambitious: 5.6 GW installed by 2025, 20 GW by 2030. But the reality is more nuanced. Typhoons impose considerable technical constraints on turbines. The Taiwan Strait, despite its powerful winds, presents some of the world's most challenging maritime conditions for installation and maintenance.

Solar power, meanwhile, runs up against a land availability problem. Taiwan is a mountainous island of 36,000 km², two-thirds of which is uninhabited. Agricultural land is protected, and exploitable rooftop space is limited. Solar yield, reasonable in the south, drops significantly in the industrialized north.

## TSMC: The World's Energy Achilles' Heel

The TSMC dimension transforms Taiwan's energy question into a global issue. The company already consumes around 6% of the island's total electricity. With the ramp-up of 3nm and 2nm chips, that consumption could triple by 2030. TSMC has made ambitious carbon-neutrality commitments, but these rest primarily on the purchase of renewable energy certificates — an accounting strategy that does nothing to alter the physical reality of the electricity mix.

If Taiwan were to experience a major power shortage, it would not be only the local economy that suffers: the entire global semiconductor supply chain would be thrown into jeopardy. TSMC's diversification plans — plants in Arizona, Japan, and Germany — represent only a fraction of its installed capacity in Taiwan.

## The Blockade Scenario: When Energy Becomes a Weapon

The most acute risk is not invasion, but blockade. China could, without firing a single shot, strangle Taiwan by intercepting LNG tankers in the strait. With 11 to 14 days of LNG reserves, the island would be forced into massive rolling blackouts within less than two weeks. Coal-fired plants, which hold larger stocks (around 30 days), would temporarily take over — but at the cost of unsustainable pollution and insufficient capacity.

This scenario is taken seriously by American and Taiwanese military planners. It partly explains the recent decision to build additional LNG storage capacity, but construction timelines of five to seven years render these projects useless in the face of a short-term crisis.

## Toward an Energy Doctrine Built on Resilience

Taiwan's energy security can no longer be thought of purely in terms of an optimal electricity mix. It must incorporate a dimension of strategic resilience: diversification of supply sources, decentralization of production, large-scale storage, and — however unpopular this may be for some — an honest reassessment of the nuclear option.

The question is not whether Taiwan can afford nuclear power. It is whether Taiwan can afford to go without it in a context where every day of guaranteed electricity production is a day of preserved sovereignty.
