I have a piece published in **[CommonWealth Magazine](https://english.cw.com.tw/article/article.action?id=4772)** (天下雜誌) — featured in the *Insight / Op-eds* section — on a variable too often overlooked in Taiwan's energy debate: not nuclear or renewables, but **the price of electricity itself**.

## What is CommonWealth Magazine?

**CommonWealth Magazine** (天下雜誌, *Tiānxià*) is Taiwan's definitive business and economics publication. Founded in 1981 by journalist **Diane Ying** (殷允芃) in the wake of the diplomatic rupture between Washington and Taipei, it has established itself as the most influential title in the Taiwanese media landscape on matters of macroeconomics, industry, technology, geopolitics, and society. In 2007, it became the island's first business media outlet to launch an English-language edition (*english.cw.com.tw*), aimed at giving an international readership a window into Taiwan and the Chinese-speaking world. Publishing an op-ed there means speaking directly to Taiwan's economic and political decision-makers.

## The argument: a frozen price, but an unfunded freeze

Taiwan posts the **third-lowest industrial tariffs and fifth-lowest residential tariffs in the world**. This affordability is not a market outcome: it is a policy choice, made without the financing architecture that should accompany it.

The numbers tell a clear story. Industrial users pay an average of **NT$4.27 per kilowatt-hour**, households around **NT$2.77**, while **Taipower**'s (台灣電力公司) average cost of production has hovered around **NT$3.8/kWh** since early 2025. Every kilowatt-hour sold to a household costs the utility nearly one New Taiwan dollar. In March 2026, the Electricity Tariff Review Committee nonetheless chose to freeze the average tariff at **NT$3.78/kWh**, citing volatility in the Middle East and the need to preserve industrial competitiveness.

## A debt, not a subsidy

The result of this freeze is substantial: **Taipower's cumulative losses stood at NT$417.9 billion** (approximately US$13 billion) as of late July 2025, despite several rate increases since 2022 and an after-tax profit of NT$72.9 billion in 2024. A NT$100 billion recapitalization plan was also blocked in the Legislative Yuan.

That is the crux of my argument: the gap between the tariff charged and the real cost **is not a subsidy — it is a debt**. The longer the disconnect between the price and its financing persists, the more expensive it will be to unwind.

## The perverse effect on the energy transition and AI

Cheap electricity also undermines the energy transition. Offshore wind, solar, and geothermal must compete against a regulated tariff that does not reflect the true cost of marginal fossil fuel generation. In that environment, it becomes very difficult to negotiate, at scale, the corporate power purchase agreements (CPPAs) on which the Lai Ching-te administration's 2030 climate targets depend.

And demand pressure will only intensify: driven by artificial intelligence, consumption could rise by **12 to 13% by 2030**, while the grid requires tens of billions of NT$ in investment. A frozen tariff designed to protect industry could, paradoxically, end up undermining the very thing it claims to defend.
