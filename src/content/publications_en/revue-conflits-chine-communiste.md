I have an article in **[Revue Conflits](https://www.revueconflits.com/la-chine-est-elle-communiste-autopsie-dun-marxisme-sans-proletariat/)** (no. 64, special report “China: a challenge for Europe”) titled “La Chine est-elle communiste ? Autopsie d'un marxisme sans prolétariat”.

In its organisation, the Chinese Communist Party owes more to Lenin than to Marx: vanguard, discipline, centralism, and a role that now consists in administering the country rather than revolutionising it, as the part played by Wang Huning shows. Yet it keeps one strong idea from Marx, the primacy of politics over the economy, running against the Western consensus in place since the 1980s. The blocking of Ant Group's stock market listing in 2020 and the requirement, since 2016, for state-owned enterprises to write the Party cell into their statutes bear this out.

What Beijing puts forward is a national Marxism, centred on Chinese specificity and civilisation, which no longer claims to speak to the workers of all countries. Hence my answer: in the sense of abolishing private property and of workers' internationalism, China is not communist, any more than the USSR was; in the sense of a Leninist party that draws its legitimacy from rising living standards, it is, in its own way. One paradox remains: Xi's party is closer to the Kuomintang that governed Taiwan in the 1970s and 1980s than to Mao's party.
