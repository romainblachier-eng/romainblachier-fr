I have an article in **Revue Conflits** (no. 64, July-August 2026, special report *"China, a challenge for Europe"*) on a deceptively simple question: is China still communist?

## More Lenin than Marx

What structures the CCP is not *Capital*, it is *What Is to Be Done?* — the vanguard party, iron discipline, democratic centralism, the conviction that the masses left to themselves never rise beyond trade-unionist consciousness: pure Lenin. The CCP works the way Lenin thought a revolutionary party should work, but it no longer makes revolution. It administers. Wang Huning is not a Marxist theorist in the classical sense. He is an engineer of Leninist power, adapting the organisational software without ever touching the hard core.

## And yet there is something deeply Marxist about it

The CCP carries a radical thesis: politics can and must subordinate the economy — the exact opposite of the Western consensus since the 1980s. The future Motuo dam in Tibet (potentially the largest hydroelectric installation in the world) is not the product of a private profitability calculation; it is a political decision. When Xi summoned Jack Ma in 2020 to block Ant Group's stock market listing, the message was crystal clear: the digital fiefdoms will not govern. The institution of the *Party cell* in every company — made compulsory in the statutes of state-owned enterprises since 2016 — is the institutional expression of this primacy of politics.

## An export model?

What China exports is not communism. The sinicisation of Marxism under Xi focuses on "Chinese characteristics", the millennia-old civilisation, socialist Confucianism. A Marxism rooted in national culture — and therefore not a bearer of the universality of proletarian internationalism.

> *"Xi Jinping Thought is not the Manifesto: it speaks to a Chinese nation, not to the workers of the world."*

## Answering the question

Is China communist? If we mean a country aiming for the abolition of private property, the self-emancipation of the proletariat and the Workers' International: no. By those criteria, the USSR never was either. If we mean a Leninist party that draws its legitimacy from the promise of growing prosperity: yes, in its own way.

The final paradox: Xi's CCP, through its state grip on capitalism and its top-down redistribution, looks more like the Kuomintang of the 1970s-1980s than Mao's CCP. Single party, mixed economy, civilisational nationalism, rising living standards as the source of legitimacy. The parallel is unsettling.
