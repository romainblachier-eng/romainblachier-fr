I have a feature in the **[Taipei Times](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/feat/archives/2026/11/18/2003867929)** titled “Taiwan’s small-reactor plans face an unresolved waste-disposal problem”. It appeared on page 12, in the Features section, on 18 November 2026.

## Small reactors, whatever the 28 November vote

The government is preparing for smaller reactors whatever voters decide on 28 November about the existing ones. Kao Tsu-mu (高梓木), president of the National Atomic Research Institute (國家原子能科技研究院), puts a possible decision to bring in light-water small modular reactors (SMRs) under a foreign licence in **2032**, with a first unit generating power in **2035 or 2036**.

## The Tsing Hua case

In Hsinchu, National Tsing Hua University (NTHU) has run Taiwan’s first research reactor for more than six decades; it is due to operate until 2040. The university wants to replace it with a microreactor developed with a US company: it signed an agreement with **NuCube Energy** on 19 October and is preparing a memorandum with another developer, **Antares Nuclear**. Yeh Tsung-kuang (葉宗洸), dean of the College of Nuclear Science, has opened talks with the Nuclear Safety Commission (核安會).

Yeh had expected NuCube to take back the reactor and its fuel at the end of a service life of about 12 years. The company told him that under current US policy it would not take back the fuel. Unless that policy changes, the spent fuel would stay in storage on the Hsinchu campus until a final repository in Taiwan enters operation.

The regulator will not write rules specific to microreactors, because designs vary too much: it will review an application to install one on the basis of a safety evaluation report. NTHU has not filed anything yet.

## The ownership lock

**Article 5 of the Electricity Act** (電業法) reserves nuclear generation, at any size, to companies in which the state holds more than half the capital. In 2024, KMT legislator Weng Hsiao-ling (翁曉玲) proposed amendments opening SMRs to private companies; they passed a first reading and went to committee.

For Anton Gao (高銘志), professor at NTHU’s Institute of Law for Science and Technology, the focus on ownership misses the main point. The same Article 5 already lets the regulator grant exceptions, and the rules on self-use power plants separate units below and above 2 megawatts without mentioning nuclear power. Private investors, he says, will want a clear framework before they commit.

## The back end, a blind spot

Taiwan has found no disposal site for either low-level or high-level waste, and has no dedicated statute setting the procedure for choosing a high-level waste site. A hospital can hand its low-level waste to the state by paying fees; Taipower cannot, and remains responsible for site selection, management and final disposal.

**Article 89** of the Electricity Act requires any generator with a nuclear plant to pay into a back-end fund. The rules do not say whether a private operator that has paid could, like a hospital, hand its waste to the state. If it remains responsible for managing the waste, and above all for finding a site, Gao warns, investor appetite for SMRs will drop significantly.

Yeh does not see this as the decisive obstacle: Taiwan already holds the spent fuel of all its plants, and fuel from small reactors can go to a final repository once one exists. In his view, the operator should pay.

## Those who question the principle

The Green Citizens’ Action Alliance (綠色公民行動聯盟) opposes small reactors whoever builds them. For its researcher Lin Cheng-yen (林正原), a smaller core removes neither safety risks nor waste, and dispersed private reactors would each have to protect their material against theft or attack. With the Environmental Jurists Association (環境法律人協會) and the Wild at Heart Legal Defense Association (蠻野心足生態協會), the alliance drafted its own siting and disposal bill, which provides for the free, prior and informed consent of local residents. Over the past year it was presented to the legislative caucuses of three parties, which have shown little appetite to take it forward.

The feature reads a small reactor bought to power a chip fab through the “developmental netzeroism” described in 2024 by Chou Kuei-tien (周桂田), director of National Taiwan University’s Risk Society and Policy Research Center: an industrial imperative sets the energy question, the answer is sought on the supply side, and the demand that created it is never discussed. Chou agrees with a caveat: SMRs have not yet taken shape as energy policy, and bringing them in for industrial demand has to be debated by society, civil groups included, or the state and industry will decide without social deliberation.

Lin Jun-rong (林均蓉), policy analyst at the Institute for National Defense and Security Research (國防安全研究院), places SMRs within energy resilience and national security, but not before a suitable regulatory framework exists and their financing has been assessed. Many advanced designs need high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU), whose commercial supply is dominated by Russia’s TENEX; she expects first uses in remote areas and on military bases rather than as replacements for large plants.

## Still no reform

The government has discussed small reactors and microreactors for several years, Gao notes, with little sign of any intention to reform the legal framework. He remains “highly pessimistic”.
