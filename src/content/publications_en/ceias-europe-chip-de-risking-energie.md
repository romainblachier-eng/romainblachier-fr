I have an analysis in **[CEIAS Insights](https://ceias.eu/europes-chip-de-risking-has-no-energy-chapter/)**, the journal of the Central European Institute of Asian Studies, published on 24 August 2026: “Europe's chip de-risking has no energy chapter”.

Europe knows it relies on Taiwan for most leading-edge chips. Its semiconductor policy, up to the Chips Act revision proposed in June 2026, follows that reliance through capacity, materials and equipment, yet leaves out the electricity Taiwan's fabs run on. A single leading-edge fab needs some 200 megawatts, on an island that imports 95% of its energy, will have no nuclear power again until around 2028, keeps only about eleven days of LNG in reserve and faces fast-rising demand from chipmaking and AI.

The danger lies less in a blockade than in a gradual squeeze: refused grid connections, postponed expansions, higher industrial tariffs, enough to slow the output Europe needs and push up the price of every imported chip. I suggest four measures: add Taiwanese power indicators to the monitoring planned under the Chips Act 2.0, see energy cooperation with Taiwan as a hedge for Europe's chip supply, test European industrial plans against a Taiwan held back by its electricity, and put energy on the agenda of the EU–Taiwan economic dialogue.
